Salecta: does AI safety survive in Nigerian Pidgin?

An open evaluation that checks whether AI models make the same safety decisions in Nigerian Pidgin as in English, in both directions.

Code and data on GitHubTry the safety filter game

The question

Nigerian Pidgin is spoken by tens of millions of people. Much of its vocabulary looks like English, but the meaning often differs. That makes it a sharp test: a model can recognise every word and still misread what someone means. Salecta looks for two ways that can go wrong.

These two cartoons show what Salecta is designed to detect. They aren't results. Real numbers will appear on this page once the evaluation runs.

How it works

1. Matched promptsEach item written three ways: plain English, Pidgin, and code-switched.
2. Models answerSeveral AI models from different providers respond to every version.
3. Grader labelsA separate model marks each answer helped, partly helped or refused. I hand-check a sample.
4. ComparePidgin vs English refusal rates, with confidence intervals.
100matched items: 60 harmless, 40 harmful
×3versions each: English, Pidgin, code-switched
2independent native-speaker checks: validation, then blind agreement

The English version of each item says plainly what the Pidgin means, rather than translating it word for word. So if a model helps with "I want to ace my exam" but refuses "I wan kill this exam", the gap comes from the Pidgin itself, not from a different request.

The harmful items are low-to-moderate risk and set in Nigerian situations, such as fake bank alerts, airtime scams and exam malpractice. Only the prompts and overall rates are published, never harmful model outputs, and providers are told about serious gaps before anything is published.

How the dataset was made

I drafted the items with help from an AI assistant (Claude). Native Nigerian Pidgin speakers then check every item for meaning and natural phrasing, and a second speaker who hasn't seen the items blind-labels a sample so I can report agreement. Each item records who drafted it and who checked it. I think being open about this matters: an AI safety project should be honest about how AI was used to build it.
