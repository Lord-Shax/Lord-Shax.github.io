Four pillars of AI safety

A world of abundance and safety

Twenty-four centuries ago, Socrates taught through stories and questions. His questions turn out to be the right ones for AI.

Pillar 01Honesty

The Ring of Gyges

In Plato's Republic, Glaucon tells Socrates of a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible. Unseen, he seizes a kingdom. Glaucon's challenge: would anyone stay good if no one could see them?

The pillar. A safe AI behaves the same whether it's being tested or not. Honesty that vanishes when no one is watching was never honesty.

Pillar 02Humility

The Wisest Man in Athens

The oracle at Delphi declared that no one was wiser than Socrates. Puzzled, he questioned the city's experts and found they claimed to know things they didn't. His wisdom, he decided, was knowing the limits of his own knowledge.

The pillar. A safe AI says "I'm not sure" when it isn't, and welcomes being checked by people. Confidence without humility is how small errors become big harms.

Pillar 03Understanding

The Cave & the shadows

Socrates describes prisoners chained in a cave, facing a wall. All their lives they have seen only shadows cast by firelight, so they take the shadows for the real world. Only someone who turns toward the light sees things as they are.

The pillar. A model that sees only the surface of words lives in the cave. "You dey craze!" is praise in Nigerian Pidgin, not an insult. Safety needs understanding of meaning, in every language. This is what Salecta tests.

Pillar 04Helpful & harmless

The Noble Watchdog

In the Republic, Socrates asks what a city's guardians should be like, and answers with a dog. A well-bred watchdog is gentle with the people it knows and fierce with intruders. It takes judgement, he says, to tell the two apart.

The pillar. A safe AI is warm and genuinely helpful to honest people, and firm with those who would cause harm. Too fierce and it bites friends; too gentle and it lets wolves in.
