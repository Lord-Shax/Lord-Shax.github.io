Can you trust a radar satellite image you didn’t take?

Satellite radar (SAR) sees through cloud and at night, so it is used for floods, ships, borders and ground motion. But most users buy images from someone else. This draft extends a method for proving optical satellite images are genuine to radar, which works very differently.

Read the draft (PDF) →

Why radar is different

Crosby and Brenning (Sandia, SmallSat 2022) proposed a tamper-proof module that signs each optical picture the moment the camera takes it, plus tests to check the satellite is honest. They left radar for future work. Radar breaks their assumptions in three ways:

  • It makes its own light. The satellite sends radio pulses and records the echoes, so you can’t test it by flashing a lamp at it.
  • The raw data is not a picture. Echoes only become an image after heavy processing on the ground, so signing the raw data doesn’t cover the image you receive.
  • Phase matters. Tiny phase edits can fake millimetre ground movement without changing how the image looks.

What the paper proposes

  • Sign the raw echoes in small pieces (a Merkle tree), so anyone can check any piece without downloading everything.
  • Spot-check the image: re-compute a few hundred random pixels from the signed echoes. If the provider edited the picture, the numbers won’t match.
  • A secret-code transponder: a box on the ground echoes the satellite’s pulses back with a secret delay and frequency shift, so its bright spot lands in a place only the user can predict. A fake or replayed image can’t show it.
  • Let the echoes check the metadata: the echoes reveal where the antenna really pointed and how fast it moved.
  • A new, seventh trust requirement: processing integrity.

Limits, honestly

Everything is simulated on a small, scaled-down scene. Edits smaller than the normal difference between two honest processors (about 0.5 dB) slip through. The next steps are real raw data from public radar missions, full-size numbers for the download cost, and a test against radar jamming.
